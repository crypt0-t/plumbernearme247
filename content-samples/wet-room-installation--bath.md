## Wet Room Installation in Bath

Bath's Georgian townhouses and terraced properties were built for a different era of bathing entirely. Many still have bathrooms squeezed into converted bedrooms or awkward half-landings, with layouts that make conventional shower enclosures impractical. A wet room solves this neatly — particularly in the narrow, tall properties common along streets like Lansdown Road or in the Oldfield Park area, where every square metre of floor space matters and a frameless, open layout genuinely transforms how a bathroom functions.

## Why Bath Properties Need Professional Wet Room Installation

The plumbing infrastructure beneath Bath's honey-coloured stone facades tells a complicated story. Many Georgian properties in the city — particularly those across the central conservation zones around the Circus and Royal Crescent — still have sections of original or early-replacement lead pipework, often combined with various generations of copper added piecemeal over the decades. Water pressure in these properties can be inconsistent, especially on upper floors of the taller townhouses, which directly affects how a wet room shower performs.

Installing a wet room in a listed property adds another layer of complexity. Bath has one of the highest concentrations of listed buildings in England, and any structural work — including cutting into floors or walls for drainage — requires careful consideration under permitted development rules. For Grade I or Grade II listed properties, you may need listed building consent before work begins, and the local planning authority will want to know that the installation won't compromise original fabric or features.

The subfloor situation in Georgian properties also warrants attention. Many have suspended timber floors rather than solid concrete, which affects how drainage is routed and how the tanking system is installed. Getting this right from the outset prevents damp problems further down the line — something that matters considerably when you're working with Bath stone walls that absorb moisture readily.

## What's Involved

A wet room installation is a multi-stage project rather than a straightforward swap-out. Here's how it typically unfolds:

**Survey and planning** — The first step is assessing the existing bathroom layout, subfloor construction, current pipe routes, and drainage fall. In Bath's older properties, this stage occasionally turns up surprises, so it's worth allowing time for a thorough inspection before any work begins.

**Strip-out** — Existing sanitaryware, flooring, and wall coverings are removed. Any pipework that needs rerouting is addressed at this stage.

**Drainage installation** — A linear or central drain is set into the floor, with the subfloor built up or adjusted to achieve the correct gradient towards it. In properties with suspended timber floors, this involves additional structural work.

**Tanking** — The entire floor and lower walls are waterproofed using a tanking membrane system. This is non-negotiable and must be done correctly before any tiling begins.

**Tiling and finishing** — Wall and floor tiles are laid, fixtures fitted, and the shower system installed.

From start to finish, a standard wet room conversion in a Bath property typically takes five to eight working days, though listed building consent requirements or unforeseen structural issues can extend this.

## Wet Room Installation Costs in Bath

Wet room installations in Bath generally fall between **£6,000 and £12,000**, with most projects sitting somewhere in the middle of that range. The final figure depends on the size of the room, the complexity of the drainage solution, and the quality of materials specified.

Bath-specific factors that push costs higher include restricted access — many central properties have no off-street parking and narrow street access that complicates materials delivery — and the additional care required when working in listed buildings. Properties with suspended timber floors rather than solid bases also require more preparation work before tanking can begin. At the upper end of the range, you're typically looking at larger rooms with premium tile choices, underfloor heating, and high-specification shower systems.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Do I need listed building consent to install a wet room in my Bath Georgian property?

If your property is Grade I or Grade II listed, you'll likely need listed building consent for any structural alterations, including cutting into floors or removing original features. It's worth checking with Bath and North East Somerset Council before work begins — your installer should be familiar with this process.

### Q: My townhouse has suspended timber floors — does that rule out a wet room?

Not at all, but it does require a different approach. Rather than cutting down into a solid concrete base, the floor level is typically built up using a purpose-made former or a screed system that achieves the correct drainage gradient. It adds time and some cost, but it's a well-established solution in Bath properties.

### Q: Will a wet room work on the upper floors of a tall Georgian terraced house where water pressure drops?

Pressure is worth testing before committing to a particular shower system. Many upper-floor bathrooms in Bath's taller terraces benefit from a pump-assisted shower or a pressurised system. A proper survey will identify whether your existing supply pressure is adequate or whether a booster is needed.

### Q: How do wet rooms hold up in Bath's older properties — is damp a concern long-term?

Tanking is the critical factor here. A properly tanked wet room, installed to current standards, should not cause damp issues even in a Bath stone building. The problems arise when tanking is cut short or done poorly. Given how absorbent Bath stone can be, it's worth asking specifically how the tanking system will be applied before any work starts.
